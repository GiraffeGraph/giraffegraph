\section{Quoted Correctly, Extracted Differently: Measuring the Checks
Between a Language Model and a Knowledge Graph on Hadith Narrator
Chains}\label{quoted-correctly-extracted-differently-measuring-the-checks-between-a-language-model-and-a-knowledge-graph-on-hadith-narrator-chains}

Yahya Efe Kuruçay, Deniz Büyükşahin, Alperen Ulukaya

Department of Computer Engineering, Akdeniz University, Antalya, Türkiye

Preprint draft, 13 October 2026. Not peer reviewed.

\textbf{Abstract.} Systems that build knowledge graphs with large
language models commonly ask the model for a source quotation and a
confidence score, and use them to decide which extracted facts to trust.
We test that practice inside a working extraction engine on the narrator
chains (isnad) of Arabic hadith texts. The engine treats the model as a
proposer: every candidate carries a quotation, deterministic checks
route doubtful candidates to human review, and each accepted fact has a
recorded acceptance. On 300 hadiths from the six canonical collections,
scored against platform-derived narrator labels, a model given only a
schema returns quotations that are all found in the source, so a
quotation check flags nothing. A check that the extracted name occurs
inside the model\textquotesingle s own quotation flags 9.2\% of its
proposals, and these contain 122 of the 145 proposals that do not match
the reference. Most of them are not invented narrators: the model
transliterated the name, changed its grammatical case, or replaced a
kinship reference by a name. At a comparable share of proposals sent to
review, the model\textquotesingle s confidence finds 50 unmatched
proposals where the check finds 122. Adding type descriptions and a few
domain hints to the prompt reduces unmatched proposals from 145 to 5 and
leaves the checks little to find. A rule-based parser without any model
call is competitive under loose matching and clearly worse under strict
matching. The reference labels are not a human gold standard, no
reviewer took part in the runs, and one model produced usable results;
we state what follows from the measurements and what does not.

\section{1 Introduction}\label{introduction}

Extraction with large language models has made it cheap to turn text
into graph facts. A model receives a passage and a schema and returns
nodes and relations. The difficulty is not producing facts but deciding
which of them may enter a graph that people will rely on. Two safeguards
are common. The model is asked to quote the source for each fact, and it
is asked how confident it is. A quotation is meant to show where a fact
came from; a confidence score is meant to select the facts a person
should check.

We examine both safeguards in a setting where a wrong fact has
consequences and where labelled data exists. A hadith opens with its
isnad, the chain of people through whom the report was transmitted: "A
told us, from B, from C". Scholars judge a hadith from the people in its
chain, so one wrong name changes the judgement. Fine-tuned models
already extract narrators and their links well when large labelled
training sets are available {[}1{]}, a knowledge graph of the six
canonical collections exists {[}2, 3{]}, and narrator names have been
linked to biographical databases {[}4{]}. We do not try to improve on
that work. We ask a different question: when a general-purpose model
proposes facts, which checks between the model and the graph find the
proposals that depart from the text, and what do they cost?

Our contributions are the following.

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  An open engine in which a model can only propose, deterministic checks
  route candidates to review with a reason code, and every accepted fact
  has an acceptance record that names a reviewer or an automatic policy
  (Section 3).
\item
  A measurement, made inside that engine on 300 unseen hadiths, of what
  two inexpensive checks flag: quotation-in-source and
  value-in-quotation. The first flags nothing. The second flags 84\% of
  the proposals that do not match the reference labels (Section 5.2).
\item
  A classification of what the flagged proposals are. Under these labels
  most "errors" of a schema-only model are unrecorded transformations of
  a correct name, not fabricated narrators.
\item
  A comparison of the check with the model\textquotesingle s confidence
  at equal review budgets (Section 5.3), and of four extraction
  conditions run through one pipeline with paired bootstrap intervals
  (Section 5.1).
\item
  A baseline that quantifies how often a written name stands for more
  than one narrator in labelled chains (Section 5.6).
\end{itemize}

\section{2 Related work}\label{related-work}

\textbf{Hadith corpora and extraction.} Sanadset 650K provides 650,986
records with narrator tags produced by regular expressions and text
matching; its data paper reports no accuracy measurement {[}5{]}. The
Ifta Sunnah data family restructures the markup of an official hadith
platform into narrator spans, transmission relations and narrator
identifiers, and fine-tuned models trained on it reach, according to the
author\textquotesingle s model repository, F1 0.98 for narrators and
0.95 for relation pairs {[}1{]}. Earlier work located isnad spans in
long unpunctuated texts and showed how much the matching rule matters:
the same CRF output scored F1 0.254 under exact and 0.835 under partial
matching, and experts agreed exactly on the end of an isnad in 53\% of
cases {[}6{]}. Segmentation of isnad from report text with n-grams
reached 92.5\% on 240 hadiths {[}7{]}.

\textbf{Narrator identity.} AR-Sanad 280K frames narrator disambiguation
as classification over 18,298 narrators. On 27,056 real chains its best
model reaches micro F1 83.5 while 60.6\% of chains contain at least one
wrong narrator {[}8{]}. A recent pipeline links 185,216 name variants to
two biographical databases without an evaluation against labelled links
{[}4{]}.

\textbf{Hadith knowledge graphs and LLMs.} SemanticHadith publishes an
ontology and graph for the six collections; its extension extracts
entities with a custom NER model and relies on experts checking a sample
of 100 instances per collection, and it lists a framework for expert
validation of ambiguous names as future work {[}3{]}. LLM-based
processing of hadith corpora has been evaluated with expert scores on a
0 to 10 scale {[}9{]} or by one author comparing 100 records with the
source {[}10{]}. A critical review names weak benchmark comparability
and sparse expert validation as open problems of the field {[}11{]}.

\textbf{Checking extracted facts.} Text2KGBench measures, besides
precision and recall against reference triples, whether the subject and
object of a triple occur in the sentence, and reports subject
hallucination of 0.17 next to precision of 0.38 for one model and
dataset: presence in the text does not imply correctness {[}12{]}.
Attribution and factuality metrics for generated text work at the level
of sentences or atomic claims {[}13, 14{]}. Self-reported confidence of
language models has been studied as a calibration problem {[}15, 16{]}.
We did not find a study that measures, for knowledge graph extraction,
the separate effect of a check on the relation between a
model\textquotesingle s quotation and the value it extracted. Our search
was not systematic.

\section{3 System}\label{system}

GiraffeGraph Core is a schema-first extraction engine with a FastAPI
backend, PostgreSQL for metadata, Neo4j for the committed graph and a
web dashboard. A project defines node types, relation types, their
properties and the properties that form the identity of a node or
relation. A source text is ingested as a source unit and cut into
segments. A provider, which may be a language model or a rule-based
program, proposes candidate nodes and relations for the segments; each
candidate carries one or more quotations with a segment reference.
Validation then assigns each candidate a status: validated, needs
review, or rejected. A run can be committed to the graph only when no
candidate of the run waits for review.

\textbf{Checks.} Three checks concern us here. (i) \emph{Schema}: a
candidate whose type is not in the schema is dropped on its own, the
other candidates of the call survive, and the unknown type is recorded
as a review item; undeclared property keys are removed and recorded the
same way. (ii) \emph{Quotation in source}: the
candidate\textquotesingle s quotation must occur in the text of the
segment it cites. (iii) \emph{Value in quotation}: a string value that
forms the identity of a node, such as a narrator\textquotesingle s name,
must occur in one of the candidate\textquotesingle s quotations.
Comparisons ignore Arabic vowel marks and letter variants. A candidate
that fails check (ii) or (iii) is routed to review whatever its
confidence, and is not rejected, because a failed check does not show
that the fact is false.

\textbf{Acceptance record.} A review decision is stored with the
reviewer\textquotesingle s name, the time, the time spent and, for an
edit, the value before and after. Decisions are appended, never
overwritten, and later re-validation does not change a candidate a
reviewer has decided. For each committed fact the engine reports either
the human decision or the automatic policy that accepted it.

\textbf{Relation identity.} A relation type can declare identity
properties. For the chain relation these are the hadith reference, the
chain number and the position, so that two transmissions between the
same two narrators in different hadiths are two edges. Without this, the
second transmission overwrote the first.

\textbf{Case-study components.} Three components are specific to hadith:
a rule-based segmenter that finds the chain span from a lexicon of 26
transmission formulas and splits it at the chain-switch marker, a schema
template (narrator, hadith, "narrated from", "transmits") with short
extraction hints, and a provider that turns the
segmenter\textquotesingle s output into candidates without any model
call.

\section{4 Experimental setup}\label{experimental-setup}

\textbf{Data.} The test set contains 300 hadiths, 50 from each of the
six canonical collections, drawn with a fixed seed from the Ifta data
after removing every record whose text matches one of 50 hadiths used
during development {[}17{]}. The reference labels are the narrator
mentions marked on the platform, 1,778 in total, between 2 and 11 per
hadith (median 6); 74 hadiths contain more than one chain. The labels
are platform markup restructured by a third party, not annotations by
independent annotators, and they are inconsistent in places: narrators
inside a compiler\textquotesingle s comment are sometimes marked and
sometimes not. For 253 of the 300 hadiths a matching record exists in
Sanadset; its regular-expression labels agree with the Ifta labels at F1
0.959 under loose matching (95\% interval 0.943 to 0.972).

\textbf{Scoring.} The unit of analysis is the hadith. System and
reference mentions are aligned by maximum-weight bipartite matching.
Under \emph{strict} matching two mentions match when their normalised
strings are equal; under \emph{loose} matching also when one contains
the other or their token F1 is at least 0.60. Precision, recall and F1
are micro-averaged. Intervals are 95\% percentile bootstrap intervals
over 2,000 resamples of hadiths, and differences between conditions use
paired resamples. A proposal is called \emph{unmatched} when it is not
aligned to a reference mention under loose matching. Unmatched is a
statement about the labels\textquotesingle{} surface form, not about
truth.

\textbf{Conditions.} All conditions run through the same engine with no
review decision.

Three points qualify these numbers. First, most unmatched proposals name
the right narrator. Seventy-nine are Latin transliterations, all from 14
hadiths in which the model switched script, and 21 are a change of
grammatical case. The same case change is unmatched on a two-word name
and matched on a longer one, because of the token threshold of the loose
rule; the check and the scorer both compare surface forms, so part of
their agreement is by construction. Second, the kinship cases are
inferences the text supports: in the example the text reads "Hisham ibn
Urwa, from his father". Fifteen of the 25 names occur in the hadith
text. We found one flagged name that the text does not contain in any
form. Third, the check does not cover category errors. Ten unmatched
proposals name the Prophet as a narrator; the check flags the two
written in Latin script and misses the eight that are copied correctly.

What the check establishes is that the model wrote something other than
what it quoted and did not say so. Of the 145 unmatched proposals, 102
have a name that does not occur in the hadith text, and the check flags
all 102.

Because a flagged candidate is not accepted until a person decides,
candidates accepted without review have precision 0.985 instead of
0.914, at recall 0.849 instead of 0.868. The graph receives less until a
reviewer acts: a run with an open review item is not committed, which
holds back 110 of the 300 hadiths and leaves the committed output at
precision 0.983 and recall 0.582.

In the isnad-aware condition 5 of 1,639 proposals are unmatched and the
check flags 3 of them, with no matched proposal flagged.

\subsection{5.4 Review workload and
cost}\label{review-workload-and-cost}

The schema-only condition sends 538 candidates to review (155 nodes and
383 relations) and commits 190 of 300 hadiths without review. The
isnad-aware condition sends 16 and commits 294. With the
provider\textquotesingle s list prices of 6 October 2026, extraction
costs USD 1.87 per 1,000 hadiths in the schema-only condition and USD
2.29 in the isnad-aware condition, at a median latency of 11 to 13
seconds per call. All runs in this paper cost USD 3.49. We did not
measure human review time.

\subsection{5.5 A failed run with a second
model}\label{a-failed-run-with-a-second-model}

We ran a larger model of the same family, Claude Sonnet 5.5, with the
same settings on 36 of the hadiths. In the isnad-aware condition it
returned a single narrator for 28 of them and usually wrote the
narrator\textquotesingle s name into the field for a
property\textquotesingle s key. Loose recall was 0.237 against 0.916 for
Haiku 5.5 on the same hadiths. We suspect two causes in our
configuration, an ambiguous wire format in which both the list entry and
the property are called \texttt{name}, and a reasoning setting that is
not calibrated alike across models; we did not test either. The run is
not a comparison of models. It does show the engine failing safely: 29
unknown property keys were recorded for review, nodes without an
identity value were rejected, 30 of 36 runs were blocked from commit,
and the precision of what was proposed stayed at 0.966 or above.

\subsection{5.6 A written name is not an
identity}\label{a-written-name-is-not-an-identity}

The engine identifies a narrator node by the written name. The Ifta
labels attach a person identifier to each chain position, which lets us
measure the consequence without running a system. The 300 hadiths have
2,150 chain positions filled by 740 people. Grouping positions by the
name exactly as written gives 964 groups; 24 of them cover more than one
person and contain 10.0\% of the positions, and 25.8\% of the people are
split over several groups (pairwise precision 0.737, recall 0.559;
B-cubed 0.941 and 0.715). Normalising the name reduces splitting
(21.8\%) and increases conflation (12.2\% of positions). The most
ambiguous name is a kinship reference, "his father", which stands for 34
people.

\section{6 Discussion}\label{discussion}

\textbf{The quotation is not where the model departs from the text.} A
quotation requirement is satisfied by pointing at real text. In our runs
the model did that every time and then wrote a value that the quoted
text does not contain. A check on the quotation alone therefore says
nothing about the value. Checking the value against the quotation costs
one more string comparison and was the only signal that separated
matched from unmatched proposals.

\textbf{Flagged is not false.} One flagged name in five matched the
reference, and most unmatched ones were the right person in another
form. A check of this kind should route to a person or to an explicit
rule, not reject. A rule that accepts changes of grammatical case and
records that it did so would clear 13 of the 33 matched names that were
flagged.

\textbf{Instructions matter more than checks, and checks matter when
instructions fail.} A few short hints and the type descriptions removed
almost all unmatched proposals, more than any check found. The checks
earn their place in the cases where the prompt, the model or the
configuration is wrong, as the failed run illustrates.

\textbf{Strict acceptance moves work to the reviewer.} Keeping a whole
hadith out of the graph while one candidate waits for review protects
the graph and leaves it incomplete: recall 0.582 in the schema-only
condition before any review. The design is only as good as the review
step, which we have not measured.

\section{7 Limitations}\label{limitations}

The reference is platform markup, not a gold standard produced by
independent annotators, and absolute scores should not be read as
accuracy. The share of unmatched proposals that the value-in-quotation
check flags is specific to this task and prompt: on 300 abstracts of the
human-annotated BC5CDR corpus {[}18{]}, with the same model and a
schema-only prompt, the check flagged 3.9\% of 2,269 proposals and these
held 53 of the 303 that did not match the annotations, while the
model\textquotesingle s confidence found 58 at the same review share;
most unmatched proposals there were verbatim copies of spans the
annotators had not labelled. No reviewer took part, so the human part of
the loop is implemented and tested but not evaluated. All usable results
come from one model, one setting and one run per condition. An unmatched
proposal is not necessarily a false statement. Narrator mentions were
scored, not relations. The hadiths are well known and are probably in
the model\textquotesingle s training data; we did not test memorisation
with altered names. Schema drift, which motivated the type and property
checks, was almost absent with the model that worked, so its frequency
in normal use is not established. The rule-based segmenter was tuned on
50 hadiths that are excluded from the test set.

\section{8 Conclusion}\label{conclusion}

Inside a working extraction engine and on 300 unseen hadiths, a check
that the extracted value occurs in the model\textquotesingle s own
quotation flagged most proposals of a weakly instructed model that do
not match the reference, while a check that the quotation occurs in the
source flagged none and the model\textquotesingle s confidence found far
fewer at the same review budget. What the check finds are mostly
unrecorded transformations of correct names. Better instructions made
the problem nearly disappear for this model. The next steps are a
human-labelled reference, a reviewer in the loop with measured review
time, more models, relation-level scoring, and linking narrator names to
an authority list.

AI assistants wrote most of the code of the evaluated system, the
analysis scripts and a first draft of this text under the
authors\textquotesingle{} direction. Bibliographic records were
retrieved from indexes or publishers\textquotesingle{} pages. Every
measured number is produced by a script from stored model responses. A
draft was checked against the result files by an independent AI
reviewer, and the claims were narrowed where the files did not support
them. The authors are responsible for the content.
